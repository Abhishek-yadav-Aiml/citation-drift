A Preliminary Study on Scientific Claim Verification to Identify Citation Drift in AI-Generated Literature Summaries

ABSTRACT

We have previously identified that literature summaries generated by some Artificial Intelligence (AI) systems may include incomplete or incorrect scientific claims connected to misleading or incorrect citations. In this preliminary study, we will develop and evaluate a basic method to identify ‘citation drift’ in literature summaries created by AI systems.  To do this, we have used the three-way natural language inference (NLI) cross-encoder (cross-encoder) model to predict either contradiction, entailment or neutral relationship between claim-evidence pairs using the development set of the SciFact dataset (n = 338).  Predictions of entailment have been mapped to SUPPORT while predictions of contradiction have been mapped to CONTRADICT; otherwise predictions have been classified as NEUTRAL.  When we consider the prediction of NEUTRAL to be incorrect, the overall accuracy of our model was 45.56%.  Our model, however, was only required to predict non-NEUTRAL direction (i.e., either SUPPORT or CONTRADICT) for 50.30% of the test cases, for which 90.59% were correct (macro F1 score = 0.91). In summary, of the test cases where our model predicted either SUPPORT or CONTRADICT, it was very accurate, however, in nearly half of the cases we were not able to discriminate between claim and evidence.preliminary component-level baseline and does not determine citation drift detection performance.

KEYWORDS
Citation drift; scientific claim verification; natural language inference; literature summarization; SciFact; factual consistency

1. INTRODUCTION
The use of large language models (LLMs) to summarize scientific papers is becoming more prevalent. Along with summaries of the methods and results of the studies included in the papers, users of LLMs may also expect summaries to describe potential limitations of the studies, as well as to provide a synthesis of related studies conducted in the same research area. However, when synthesizing information across several studies, it is not uncommon for the summary to contain inaccuracies (e.g. includes/excludes qualifiers to/states/the result differently/more vaguely than in the original study).  Such inconsistencies provide the motivation for this work to study citation drift.

Reliably determining the accuracy of claims in scientific literature is closely related to natural language inference (NLI). In such cases, the summarized claim is the hypothesis and the supporting evidence the premise.  However, compared to other fields, the use of scientific terminology and/or evidence in support of claims incorporated in the text greatly increases the complexity of the task.

We present here a preliminary study using an NLI model pre-trained on the SciFact dataset to classify claim pairs from the datasets we analyzed.

The experiment has the following main aspects: first, we use a pretrained DeBERTa-v3 Natural Language Inference (NLI) cross-encoder to assess pairs of scientific claims and evidence. Second, as opposed to previous studies, we take into consideration the 3 classes (neither of which are defined in this work) associated with the output of the model, as opposed to the 2 gold (i.e. human-annotated) labels of this evaluation task. Third, we report both overall accuracy of predictions, as well as conditional accuracy, thus allowing the reader to assess the contribution of ‘neutral’ predictions of the model.

2. RELATED WORK

2.1. Scientific Claim Verification
Scientific claim verification gives the researchers the means to automatically check if evidence in the research literature supports or refutes a given scientific claim. In their work, Wadden et al. have created the SciFact dataset containing a collection of scientific claims paired with evidence from research papers (abstracts) as well as support/refutation labels and rationales.  We have used this dataset in our work for claim-evidence pair settings, and in particular, have not considered this dataset as containing summaries generated by natural language generation (NLG) systems.

2.2 Natural Language Inference
In Natural Language Inference we pose the problem of reasoning between pairs of sentences as a 3-way classification problem, i.e. deciding between entailment, contradiction and neutral cases.


2.3 Previous language models
One method for language representation learning is to use disentangled attention along with an enhanced mask decoder as proposed in DeBERTa. Recently, a newer version of this family of models, namely, DeBERTa-v3, used replaced token detection along with gradient-disentangled embedding sharing. We employ the previously trained model cross-encoder/nli-deberta-v3-base for our task of NLI (no further training is performed).

2.4 Location of this study
We are in the early stages of use of this methodology and therefore claim that we have developed a simple baseline solution to the evaluation of scientific claims using automatically generated summaries.
We have not, however, included any of the following in our current implementation: automated citation links based on the generated summaries, or manual annotations to evaluate citation drift.  To do this we would need human evaluation of each individual ‘atomic’ claim along with the associated text supporting the claim.

3. METHOD

3.1 Formulation of the Task

In each example of our dataset, we are given a scientific claim c and an evidence text e. In our particular case, we provide the evidence text e to the NLI model as the premise and the scientific claim c to the model as the hypothesis to generate a classification i.e. CONTRADICTION, ENTAILMENT or NEUTRAL.

For comparison purposes with the labels provided for the SciFact dataset, we consider ‘ENTIALMENT’ to mean ‘SUPPORT’ and ‘CONTRADICTION’ to mean ‘CONTRADICT’ respectively.




3.3 NLI Inference
We used the cross-encoder model ‘cross-encoder/nli-deberta-v3-base’ to score pairs of evidence and claims. The model outputs are mapped to the output index as follows: 0 = contradiction, 1 = entailment, and 2 = neutral.  Based on this output we predict the class for each evidence-claim pair.  We do not apply any training/further optimization to this model using the few examples available in the SciFact development set.

3.4 Evaluation Measures
We calculated overall accuracy over all the 338 examples in the development set. Further, we calculated the coverage of the set of examples for which the model returned either SUPPORT or CONTRADICT. Lastly, we computed conditional precision, recall and F1 score over this subset (of the examples returned as either SUPPORT or CONTRADICT).

 4.1 Dataset
We use SciFact to generate claims, evidence rationales, and abstracts. We created 338 claim-evidence pairs using the development split of SciFact. Of these, 216 examples are ‘Support’ and 122 are ‘Contradict’ based on our ground truth labels.

5. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
5.1 Prediction Distribution
The model produces 76 SUPPORT predictions, 94 CONTRADICT predictions, and 168 NEUTRAL predictions. Therefore, it makes a non-neutral prediction for 170 out of 338 examples, giving coverage of 50.30%.

5.2 Overall Evaluation
The full confusion matrix, with gold labels as rows and predicted labels as columns, is:

                    Predicted SUPPORT   Predicted CONTRADICT   Predicted NEUTRAL
Gold SUPPORT                 73                    13                  130
Gold CONTRADICT               3                    81                   38

The overall accuracy is 45.56%, calculated as 154 correct predictions out of 338. Neutral predictions are counted as incorrect because the gold-label set contains only SUPPORT and CONTRADICT.

5.3 Conditional Evaluation
When neutral predictions are excluded, 170 examples remain. The model correctly classifies 154 of them, giving conditional accuracy of 90.59%. The conditional confusion matrix is:

                    Predicted SUPPORT   Predicted CONTRADICT
Gold SUPPORT                 73                    13
Gold CONTRADICT               3                    81

For this subset, the reported classification metrics are:
- CONTRADICT: precision 0.86, recall 0.96, F1-score 0.91, support 84.
- SUPPORT: precision 0.96, recall 0.85, F1-score 0.90, support 86.
- Macro average: precision 0.91, recall 0.91, F1-score 0.91.
- Weighted average: precision 0.92, recall 0.91, F1-score 0.91.

5.4 Discussion
The main observation is a trade-off between prediction coverage and conditional accuracy. The model makes a non-neutral prediction for approximately half of the examples. Within that subset, its predictions agree with the gold labels in 90.59% of cases. However, the conditional score applies only to the 170 non-neutral predictions. Reporting it without the 50.30% coverage would overstate performance across the full dataset.

The 168 neutral predictions may reflect uncertainty or a mismatch between the model's NLI decision boundary and the way evidence is presented. The current measurements do not establish the cause. The results suggest that a pretrained NLI model can provide a useful starting point for scientific claim verification, but further analysis is required before using it as a citation-verification component.

6. LIMITATIONS
This study has several limitations. First, SciFact is a scientific claim-verification dataset, not a dataset of AI-generated literature summaries and their citations. Second, the experiment uses the development split and does not report an independent test-set evaluation. Third, the gold annotations contain two classes, while the model produces three classes. Neutral predictions are counted as incorrect for overall accuracy, and this treatment affects the interpretation of the result. Fourth, conditional accuracy applies only to non-neutral predictions and must be reported with coverage. Fifth, exact runtime configuration, package versions, model revision, and some dataset-release details are not included in the available experiment record. Finally, this experiment does not evaluate retrieval quality, evidence aggregation across multiple sources, atomic claim decomposition, or human annotator agreement.

7. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
This paper presents a preliminary scientific claim-verification baseline using a pretrained DeBERTa-v3 NLI cross-encoder on 338 SciFact development claim–evidence pairs. The model achieves 45.56% overall accuracy when neutral predictions are counted as incorrect. It produces non-neutral predictions for 50.30% of the examples and achieves 90.59% conditional accuracy on that subset. These results show that coverage should be reported alongside conditional performance when a model has a neutral output that is not represented in the gold-label set.

The experiment is a component-level baseline relevant to citation-support assessment. It is not a direct measurement of citation drift in AI-generated literature summaries. Future work should construct a benchmark from AI-generated summaries, break summaries into atomic claims, connect each claim to its cited source, retrieve relevant source passages, and obtain human-verified support labels. The evaluation should also distinguish genuinely unsupported claims from cases where relevant evidence is unavailable or not retrieved.
